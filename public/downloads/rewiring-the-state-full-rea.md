# Rewiring the State: implementation evidence note

**Assessment date:** 9 August 2026  
**Public framing revised:** 10 August 2026  
**Primary policy context:** *Rewiring the State: Cabinet Statement*, 31 July 2026  

## Purpose

The Cabinet Statement establishes a direction of travel toward greater devolution, integration and changes in how the state operates. This evidence note does **not** assess the political desirability of that direction or recommend for or against it.

It asks a narrower implementation question:

> **Given the stated direction of travel, what conditions, assumptions and indicators should be monitored if the expected benefits are to materialise?**

The core principle is that **expected benefits are hypotheses to manage, not assumptions to bank**.

## How to read the evidence

For each expected-benefit pathway, separate six things:

1. the expected benefit;
2. the mechanism through which it is expected to arise;
3. the conditions needed for that mechanism to operate;
4. leading indicators that can be observed before final outcomes move;
5. final outcomes, costs and distributional effects; and
6. the management response if evidence develops differently from expectation.

Evidence confidence is therefore not a score for whether a policy is "good" or "bad". It indicates how much confidence the public evidence gives us about a particular link in the theory of change and how intensively that link should be monitored or tested during implementation.

## Evidence base

The rapid assessment searched academic and official sources on 9 August 2026. The screening log records:

- 58 records entered;
- 6 duplicates removed;
- 52 unique records screened;
- 45 full texts or official pages assessed; and
- 39 sources included.

Sources were appraised separately for design quality, directness to England, relevance to the specific pathway and transferability from other institutional settings. The assessment is rapid rather than exhaustive: bundled reforms, short follow-up periods, inconsistent measures and institutional differences limit causal attribution and transferability.

## Expected-benefit pathways

### 1. Local decision-making → better tailoring and coordination

**Evidence confidence: Moderate.**

Public evidence supports the importance of local information and cross-sector coordination, while showing that authority, capability and relationships affect whether proximity translates into better delivery.

**Monitor:** clarity of decision rights; cross-boundary coordination; local analytical and delivery capability.

### 2. Coherent local powers and investment → stronger local growth

**Evidence confidence: Moderate, with mixed estimates.**

English and international estimates vary. Some English programmes show gains while other designs find limited or uneven effects. This makes institutional maturity, functional geography, additionality and local economic conditions important implementation variables rather than background assumptions.

**Monitor:** productivity and employment separately; additionality and displacement; distribution of gains within the functional region.

### 3. Greater alignment across services → better access and outcomes

**Evidence confidence: Moderate.**

Evidence is more consistently positive for access, user experience and selected population-health outcomes than for system-wide costs or cashable savings. Greater Manchester provides important direct evidence, but its maturity and wider reform package constrain simple transfer.

**Monitor:** access and user experience; population outcomes; total system cost and where costs move between organisations.

### 4. Earlier intervention → lower later harm and stronger public value

**Evidence confidence: Moderate for selected interventions.**

Some preventive programmes generate substantial long-run benefits. That does not by itself establish that a particular governance arrangement will shift resources upstream or generate cashable savings.

**Monitor:** lead indicators before final outcomes move; time to benefit; which organisation or budget captures later benefits.

### 5. Greater revenue responsibility → stronger incentives and funding certainty

**Evidence confidence: Moderate and design-dependent.**

Revenue autonomy can strengthen the link between local decisions and resources, but volatility, unequal tax bases and risks outside local control mean that equalisation and risk-sharing are part of the mechanism.

**Monitor:** revenue volatility; exposure to shocks outside local control; equalisation and reset effects on incentives.

### 6. Local flexibility plus redistribution → fairer spatial outcomes

**Evidence confidence: Low–Moderate.**

The evidence does not justify assuming a single distributional effect. Strong institutions and redistribution can limit divergence, while English evidence also indicates that gains can concentrate in stronger districts or groups.

**Monitor:** within-region distribution as well as averages; comparable service capacity; who benefits from growth and investment.

### 7. Clearer responsibility and usable information → stronger accountability

**Evidence confidence: Moderate.**

Evidence suggests transparency is most useful when information is understandable, responsibility is clear, scrutiny has capacity and there are meaningful consequences. More reporting alone is not the same as stronger accountability.

**Monitor:** legibility of responsibilities; scrutiny capacity; reporting burden; metric gaming and unintended incentives.

### 8. Institutional capability → effective use of new powers and resources

**Evidence confidence: High.**

Capability is one of the most consistent findings across English audits, evaluations and international synthesis. It is better treated as an investable part of implementation than as a fixed characteristic that some places simply have or lack.

**Monitor:** analytical, commercial and digital capacity; workforce and leadership resilience; scrutiny and partnership capability.

### 9. A more strategic centre → effective local discretion and system stewardship

**Evidence confidence: Low for the effect of organisational size itself.**

Comparative evidence supports continuing central functions in standards, equalisation, interoperable data, assurance, system risk and learning. There is much less direct evidence connecting the size of the centre itself to outcomes.

**Monitor:** duplication and decision time; quality of national standards and shared data; system resilience and escalation capability.

### 10. Changing where public-body functions sit → better delivery or public value

**Evidence confidence: Very low direct evidence.**

Existing evidence is mainly about choosing and overseeing delivery models rather than estimating the effects of moving specific functions between organisations or tiers.

**Monitor:** baseline cost and performance; independence and specialist capability; transition cost; service continuity.

## Active benefits management

A practical monitoring approach is:

**Expected benefit → mechanism → enabling conditions → lead indicators → outcomes → adapt and learn**

This allows implementation to distinguish at least four situations that can otherwise look similar in final outcome data:

- the mechanism is working but the outcome takes time to materialise;
- the mechanism is working but benefits are appearing in a different organisation, group or geography;
- the mechanism is not operating because an enabling condition is missing; or
- the expected causal relationship itself is weaker than assumed.

The appropriate response may therefore be to scale, redesign, provide additional capability, change an incentive, improve data, pause an element, or remediate an implementation problem. Those are management responses to evidence, not judgements about the political desirability of the reform as a whole.

## Transferability

International evidence is useful where it identifies mechanisms or recurring implementation conditions, but it should not be treated as a single pool. Transfer judgements need to consider constitutional structure, real subnational fiscal autonomy, equalisation, geography, administrative tradition, baseline inequality, institutional quality and reform maturity.

A high-quality study can therefore provide strong evidence about a mechanism while providing only limited direct evidence about contemporary English institutional reform.

## Transparency and limitations

The accompanying evidence table contains the 39 included sources, appraisal notes and transferability judgements. The screening log records inclusion, exclusion and duplicate decisions.

This is a rapid assessment, not an exhaustive systematic review. Citation chasing was selective, no unpublished evidence call was conducted and several central questions concern bundled institutional changes that are intrinsically difficult to identify causally.

The public portfolio version is intentionally framed around evidence confidence, implementation and learning. The earlier pre-reframe version is preserved in the repository's Git history for audit and recovery rather than being treated as the current public-facing interpretation.
