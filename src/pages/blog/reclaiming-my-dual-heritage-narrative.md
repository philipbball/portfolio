*Originally shared for Black History Month under the theme “Reclaiming Narratives”. Archived here substantially as originally published; time-specific role and family references reflect the original piece.*

This year’s Black History Month theme is "Reclaiming Narratives," focusing on recognising and correcting the stories of Black history and culture.

I was born in Lesotho, in the middle of South Africa in the early 80’s during Apartheid. My dad and mom, a White English man and Black Sotho woman, fell in love and got married at a time when this was illegal in South Africa. Both sides of their families were not initially supportive.

As teachers, they committed their lives to helping those less well off access opportunities, establishing and teaching at schools in Sierra Leone, Chad, Lesotho and Swaziland. They challenged the status quo; teaching children STEM subjects and computers in the early 80s, despite being told that all those kids needed to learn was how to work the fields and put food on the table.

My dad was a progressive and change maker. Coming from a working class background, he was the first in his family to go to university, and made it to Christ Church, Oxford, at a time when it was rare for the non-public school educated. Instead of the City, he moved to Africa to help set up and run schools.

My mom was a fighter. Growing up in a rural African village, her family did not have any money to give her higher education. However she took it on herself to travel to the Capital, and knocked on enough doors to get her education sponsored by ChildHope. She always fought for justice and fairness. When the military tried to shut down their school at gunpoint during an attempted Coup, she personally confronted them unarmed, convincing them to leave.

Despite the injustice they faced personally, they always put a positive spin on things. When on their honeymoon in Botswana, on entering the showers which were filled with ‘Whites’, everyone that was there left assuming they were in the wrong place. However, the story that they told me reflected on how great it was to have the showers all to themselves.

My parents were the rocks that helped me navigate a world that wasn’t made for me - a world where I constantly questioned my identity.

Growing up in Swaziland, Southern Africa - a predominantly black autocracy ruled by a 100% Monarch, seemed normal at the time, as that was all I had ever seen. I never questioned why all the people with money were predominantly White. Why our curriculum only taught us White, Western history. Or why as teenagers the African American experience was seen as the ideal, and the African experience an ordeal we’d rather forget.

Only when coming to the UK did my eyes open to how the life I experienced was ‘engineered’ to downplay the richness of the African experience - and therefore my own.

There was a time when as a teen in South Africa I wished I was White: that was what society said good looked like. Then, when we got access to MTV and BET and I saw Black people living well in America I wished I was Black. I never realised how great it was to just be me and what my existence represented.

I also never realised how fortunate I was to have my parents, with their diverse and very different but complementary backgrounds. They showed me through their actions, how to overcome adversity and setbacks and how to look at the positive in things. They were not either or: they were both, and more.

Unfortunately, both my parents passed away due to cancer. However, their example helped me reframe this into something that is more positive by taking action and raising money for Cancer Research.

I now have a six year old daughter. As they did, I make sure I tell her how much I love her everyday. The lessons of my White father and my Black mother have helped me to be the great father that I am today, giving me the tools to raise a kind, compassionate, joyful and brave little girl who in her own way is a fighter for justice and fairness already, challenging the status quo: in life, at School and as a Girl Guide, but also on the mat having already graded in Taekwondo and Gymnastics.

So in reclaiming my dual heritage narrative I have come to realise that I am Resilient. I am strong. I don’t need to hide. Am I Black? Am I White? I am both and more.
